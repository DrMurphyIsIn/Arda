\section{Case study II: Riemann-zeta zero-free regions}
\label{sec:rh}

The second case study is in analytic number theory, and it comes with a warning
label we want to put first, in bold, so there is no chance of misreading: \textbf{we
do not claim the Riemann Hypothesis.} What we have is a small number of genuine,
unconditional, machine-checked results \emph{about} the zeta function---and an
honest account of how they sit relative to the classical literature, which is that
they are weaker.

\subsection{Four verified results}

Each of the following is proved in Lean~4 against pinned Mathlib, is
\emph{unconditional}, and is guarded in continuous integration by an axiom check
(\texttt{\#print axioms} must report no \texttt{sorryAx}), so ``verified'' here is
enforced, not asserted:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item a fractional-part \emph{strip representation} of $\zeta$ on
    $\{\operatorname{Re} s > 0\} \setminus \{1\}$ (\texttt{zeta\_fract\_repr});
  \item the sharp bound $\lVert \zeta(\sigma+it) \rVert \le 6\,(1 + \log|t|)$ on
    $1 \le \sigma \le 2,\ |t| \ge 2$, with an explicit constant
    (\texttt{zeta\_log\_bound});
  \item an elementary, Hadamard-free zero-free region
    $\operatorname{Re} s > 1 - c/|t|^5$ (\texttt{riemannZeta\_zero\_free\_poly});
  \item a sharper region $\operatorname{Re} s > 1 - c/\!\left(|t|^4 (1 + \log 2|t|)\right)$
    (\texttt{riemannZeta\_zero\_free\_polylog}).
\end{enumerate}

To our knowledge, the last two are the first unconditional, Hadamard-free
zero-free regions to be machine-checked in Lean.

\subsection{The honest comparison}

Now the part that keeps this truthful. These regions are \textbf{strictly weaker
than de la Vallée-Poussin}: the classical region is $\operatorname{Re} s > 1 -
c/\log|t|$ \cite{dlvp1896}, and $1/\log|t|$ beats a polynomial or polylog decay in
$|t|$ handily; Vinogradov--Korobov is stronger still. So we have improved nothing
about the analytic \emph{result}. What is new is the \emph{formal derivation}: an
elementary, Hadamard-free route to an unconditional region, carried out in a proof
assistant, together with a handful of reusable gap-fillers (the strip
representation and the explicit $\log$-bound among them). The contribution is the
machine-checked path, stated at its true strength---no more.

\subsection{A second criterion: Jensen--P\'olya hyperbolicity}

The zero-free regions are not the only honestly-scoped RH statement the pipeline
reaches. The Griffin--Ono--Rolen--Zagier theorem \cite{gorz2019} restates RH as the
assertion that \emph{every} Jensen--P\'olya polynomial of the Riemann
$\xi$-function is hyperbolic (real-rooted). We kernel-verify the finite, certificate-shaped
instances: three sorry-free theorems (\texttt{jensen\_box\_hyperbolic\_deg2\_\{0,1,2\}})
establishing that the degree-$2$ Jensen polynomials $J^{2,n}$ for $n = 0, 1, 2$
are hyperbolic, through a discriminant bridge lemma
(\texttt{hyperbolic\_deg2\_of\_discrim\_nonneg}), with the clean axiom set and a
per-theorem statement-match check. This is emphatically \emph{not} a proof of
RH---it checks finitely many concrete polynomials, and the uniform-in-degree
threshold that would actually close the criterion is out of scope. But it is a
second, independent, honestly-scoped kernel-verified statement about $\zeta$,
produced by the same pipeline and exhibiting yet another certificate shape (a
real-rootedness / hyperbolicity certificate rather than a positivity one).

\subsection{A third route: verifying RH inside a box}

A different question again is not whether the zeros are simple or where they are
\emph{not}, but where they concretely \emph{are}---and, more strongly, whether a
given region contains any \emph{off}-line zeros at all. This is Turing's method,
and we carry it out in the kernel. The capstone is now \emph{parameterized}: the
generic-corner theorem \texttt{RHInBox.rh\_in\_box\_of\_certificate} certifies
\emph{any} finite rational box $[\sigma_0,\sigma_1] \times [T_0,T_1]$ on demand,
establishing that every nontrivial zero of $\zeta$ inside it lies exactly on the
critical line $\operatorname{Re} s = \tfrac12$. It is assembled from two
kernel-derived halves---a corner- and count-generic counting-exhaustion
(\texttt{RHInBoxCore}) and a zeta Blaschke split whose box argument principle, over
a \emph{variable} ball, \emph{derives} the interior zero-count from the boundary
winding rather than assuming it (\texttt{RHInBoxAnalytic}). Every artifact is
sorry-free with the clean axiom set $\{$\texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice},
\texttt{Quot.sound}$\}$, compiled and drift-checked in continuous integration.

The verified \emph{range} has grown with the generalization. The original box
$[\tfrac{2}{5}, \tfrac{3}{5}] \times [10, 35]$ (five on-line zeros) is reproduced
as a byte-identical regression, and the committed milestone now reaches height
$T = 100$: the instantiation \texttt{RHInBox\_2d5\_3d5\_0\_100} certifies that all
$29$ nontrivial zeros in the box $[\tfrac{2}{5}, \tfrac{3}{5}] \times [0, 100]$ lie
exactly on the critical line---and $29$ is the full classical count of zeros up to
height $100$.

The caveats are stated as sharply as the claim. It is \emph{finite}---a box, not
all of $\mathbb{C}$; Turing's method verifies RH in a region, it does not prove
RH. And it is \emph{conditional on certified numeric input}: the boundary winding
integer, the edge non-vanishing, and the value enclosures are supplied by
high-precision interval (Arb) arithmetic and carried as explicit, non-kernel Lean
hypotheses---a documented trust boundary, exactly as with the constant enclosures
elsewhere in the system. The kernel proves \emph{every implication} from those
inputs; it does not verify the enclosures themselves. The chain is non-circular by
construction: the winding count enters as a boundary-contour value, never as
``$N$ zeros and no others,'' and ``all zeros on the line'' is the \emph{derived}
conclusion. It is, once more, emphatically \emph{not} a proof of RH---but it is a
kernel-verified RH-in-a-box for any finite box, now certified up to height $100$,
and it exhibits a third distinct certificate shape (winding / argument-principle
zero-counting).

For completeness we note two further items we handle with extra care. The first
is a genuine boundary item: a Borel--Carath\'eodory theorem is present in the
development but is a \emph{draft}---its constant is not sharp, it is not
axiom-guarded, and it is not yet wired into the region argument, so we do not lean
on it. The second has, over the course of this campaign, graduated. The classical
de~la~Vall\'ee-Poussin region has now been carried \emph{end-to-end} for a
concrete zeta zero, and it sits in the same CI-verified tier as the four results
above: a dedicated axiom guard (\texttt{AxiomGuardDlvp}) runs
\texttt{\#print axioms} on it on every commit and fails the build on any
\texttt{sorryAx}. For a zero $\rho_0 = \beta + i\gamma$ with
$\tfrac12 < \beta < 1$ and $|\gamma| \ge \tfrac72$, the theorem
\texttt{dlvp\_zeta\_region\_concrete} establishes that there exist $A > 0,\ L \ge
1$ with $\beta \le 1 - 1/(112\,AL)$, with the clean axiom set
$\{$\texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice}, \texttt{Quot.sound}$\}$. The
analytic inputs are no longer hypotheses: the pole bound and the
height-$\gamma$/height-$2\gamma$ $g$-bounds are now \emph{proved} lemmas, and the
numeric coupling that stalled the earlier draft is discharged by constructing $A$
and $L$ explicitly. One honest qualification remains, and it is different in kind
from ``conditional'': the region constant is \emph{non-effective}---$A$ and $L$
are existentially quantified---so the theorem certifies the derivation and the
\emph{existence} of the region constants without yet exposing the classical rate
$1 - c/\log|t|$ with an explicit $c$. It is a kernel-verified, CI-guarded
\emph{reduction} to a de~la~Vall\'ee-Poussin region for concrete zeros, not yet an
effective bound---and emphatically not a proof of RH. We state both items
precisely rather than let the first blur into the verified list or the second be
kept artificially out of it.

The methodological point for this paper is that the same
untrusted-generator/trusted-kernel discipline that handled a combinatorial
extremal problem also produces honest analytic results---and, just as important,
makes it easy to state exactly how strong they are and are not.
