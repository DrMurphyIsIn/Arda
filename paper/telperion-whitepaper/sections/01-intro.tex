\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Two anxieties attend the growing use of machines in mathematical proof. The first
is \emph{trust}: as large language models, SAT/SMT solvers, and semidefinite
programming (SDP) tools increasingly produce candidate proofs and lemmas, how do
we know their output is correct, and not merely plausible? The second is a
\emph{gap}: many powerful proof techniques---sum-of-squares (SOS) certificates,
Positivstellensatz representations, linear-programming duals---are discovered
numerically, in floating point, and a numerically ``verified'' certificate is not
a proof. Rounding a near-feasible SDP solution to something a proof assistant will
accept, without silently changing the claim, is itself delicate work.

\Telperion{} addresses both by committing to a strict division of labor. A large,
freely replaceable \emph{generator} searches for a certificate and serializes it
in exact rational arithmetic. A small \emph{trusted core}---\Lean's kernel, and
nothing else---re-derives the theorem from that certificate. The generator is
never trusted: if it emits a wrong certificate, the emitted \Lean{} fails to
compile; it can never yield a false theorem. This is not a slogan but a structural
property of the pipeline, and it is what lets the same tool prove Bernoulli's
inequality and drive a research-scale formalization through identical machinery.

Concretely, one describes a problem as a \emph{certifiable family}: a grid of
instances, each reducible to one of \Telperion's certificate \emph{shapes}. The
tool certifies each instance in exact arithmetic, then emits \Lean~4 that
\Mathlib's kernel re-proves from scratch. Roughly thirty shapes are supported
today---rational-function positivity by P\'olya lifting, exact SOS (reaching
interior equality cases), Artin-denominator rational SOS for nonnegative-but-not-SOS
polynomials, Putinar and Handelman certificates on semialgebraic sets, Farkas /
linear-Positivstellensatz combinations, Nullstellensatz and real-Nullstellensatz
ideal-membership certificates, Wilf--Zeilberger hypergeometric identities,
Chv\'atal--Gomory integer rounding, and more (\S\ref{sec:emitters})---and where a
statement needs a new shape, one adds an \emph{emitter}; the trust model comes
for free.

\paragraph{An independent second opinion.} Kernel acceptance already rules out
false theorems. But a single implementation---\Lean's elaborator and kernel---is
still a single point of trust, and kernel acceptance alone cannot certify that the
emitted proof proves the statement one \emph{meant}, rather than a vacuous or
weakened variant. \Telperion's output can therefore be handed to an independent
judge: the \Comparator~\cite{comparator} from OpenAI's \emph{ten-proofs}
project~\cite{tenproofs}. Given a \emph{challenge} module (the intended statement)
and a \emph{solution} module (the emitted proof), the \Comparator{} exports both
and checks three things no ordinary build can: that the solution proves
\emph{exactly} the challenge statement; that it uses only a declared whitelist of
axioms; and that it survives replay not only by \Lean's kernel but by \nanoda---an
independent reimplementation of the kernel in Rust~\cite{nanoda}. A soundness bug
would have to fool two independently written kernels (\S\ref{sec:indep}).

\paragraph{A flagship, honestly scoped.} We use a formalization of the
Brualdi--Goldwasser extremal-tree conjecture as the paper's running research
example. We state plainly, and repeatedly, what it demonstrates. The conjecture is
\emph{not} proved. \Telperion{} and the surrounding formalization have produced a
\emph{conditional} reduction of the full conjecture to two precisely stated open
layers, together with the two analytic inequalities that reduction rests on; those
theorems are sorry-free, axiom-clean, and---as we report in \S\ref{sec:bg}---accepted
by both \Lean's kernel and \nanoda. The point of the case study is not a
completeness claim; it is that the boundary between proved and open is here a
machine-checked object, not a matter of a reader's charity toward prose.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This paper makes no claim to a new decision procedure
or a new certificate class. Its contribution is an \emph{integrated system} and
the methodology it embodies:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item a certificate-compilation pipeline (\S\ref{sec:emitters}) that turns
    exact-arithmetic certificate families into kernel-checked \Lean, with a
    composable library of $\sim$30 emitter shapes and an enforced
    \code{certify $\to$ validate $\to$ emit $\to$ freeze} workflow;
  \item a trust model (\S\ref{sec:trust}) in which the generator is untrusted by
    construction and the sole trusted component is the kernel, with explicit
    guards against the one failure the kernel cannot catch---vacuity;
  \item an independent-verification layer (\S\ref{sec:indep}) that adds
    statement-identity, an axiom whitelist, and a \emph{second, non-\Lean{}
    kernel} on top of kernel acceptance; and
  \item an end-to-end demonstration on both closed textbook problems and an
    in-progress research formalization (\S\ref{sec:bg}), with the proved/open
    boundary machine-enforced.
\end{enumerate}
\Telperion{} is a standalone, problem-agnostic artifact; nothing about the engine
is specific to the case study. It is available as open source (\S\ref{sec:concl}).
